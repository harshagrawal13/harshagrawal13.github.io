Why your 8.3s all feel arbitrary

Absolute ratings ask you to hold a 60-item scale in your head at once, and the scale drifts: the 8.3 you gave in 2021 was calibrated against a different library than today's. Worse, the number does double duty — it encodes both how good and how sure you are, so you compress everything into 7–9 and the ranking inside that band is noise.

A pairwise comparison asks something you actually know: A or B. It's local, it needs no scale, and it's stable over time. The job is then to turn a pile of local judgments into one global number.

The model

This uses Bradley–Terry: every show gets a strength θ, and the chance you prefer i over j is 1 / (1 + e^-(θᵢ-θⱼ)). Given your answers, it finds the θ values that make what you actually said most likely (maximum likelihood, fit by gradient ascent, refit after every single answer).

It's the same math behind chess Elo — but Elo is an online approximation that updates one rating at a time and depends on the order games were played. Fitting the whole model at once is strictly better when, as here, you have all the data sitting in front of you and only sixty players. Order doesn't matter, and an answer you give on duel 200 correctly propagates back through everything.

Where your old ratings go

Throwing away 60 existing ratings would be wasteful — they're noisy, not worthless. They're used as a prior: the fit is pulled toward your Notion numbers, and each duel you judge pulls it back toward the evidence. Slide the prior to zero to rank purely from duels; leave it up to converge in far fewer questions. This is the practical difference between needing ~400 comparisons and needing ~120.

Which pairs you get asked

Not random. A duel between your #1 and your #58 teaches nothing — you already know the answer. The information in a comparison is p(1-p), maximal when the model thinks it's a coin flip. Adaptive mode samples candidate pairs and picks the one that is both close in current strength and involves shows it has seen least. That's why it feels hard: every question is meant to be hard. Switch to Mixed if you want easier ones mixed in.

Knowing when to stop

Each show gets a standard error from the Fisher information — how sharply the data pins its strength down. The Confidence readout is the share of your library whose error has dropped below the gap between adjacent shows. Practical stopping points:

  • ~60 duels — every show seen at least twice; the top and bottom tiers are real.
  • ~150 duels — tiers are solid, order within a tier is roughly right.
  • ~350 duels — near-total order. This is the n·log n regime; beyond it you're mostly re-measuring noise.

Turning strength back into a rating out of 10

θ is on a log-odds scale, which is correct but unreadable. Three honest translations, switchable on the Ranking tab:

  1. Matched to your old scale — take the exact multiset of numbers you already use (9.7, 9.6, 9.6, … 3.0) and re-deal them in the new order. Your scale's feel is preserved and only the ordering changes. Best for pasting back into Notion.
  2. Win % against the library — the model's average chance this show beats a random show you've watched. The most interpretable number here, and immune to scale drift: "beats 88% of everything I've seen" means the same thing forever.
  3. Raw log-odds — the underlying quantity. A gap of 1.0 means ~73/27 odds; a gap of 2.2 means ~90/10. Use it to see where the real cliffs in your taste are.
Ties are data, not a cop-out. "Too close to call" is recorded as half a win each way — it says these two belong on the same rung, which is genuine information. "Haven't seen enough" is recorded as nothing at all, and the pair won't be asked again soon.

Reading the contradictions

The disagreement list shows answers the fitted model finds most surprising — you said A beat B while everything else you've said implies B is far stronger. Some are mistakes. Some are the interesting cases where a show is genuinely great on one axis and weak on another, and a single total order is the wrong shape for your taste. If a show shows up there repeatedly, that's a signal it deserves its own category rather than a rank.

Why genre leagues are the better ladder

Ranking Chernobyl against Brooklyn Nine-Nine forces you to answer a question that doesn't really have an answer — they're not competing at the same thing. Cross-genre duels are where most of your "arbitrary" feeling actually comes from, and they're also where the model picks up the most contradictions.

Inside a league the comparison is honest, and there's a second win: a league is small. Anime is 6 shows, so 15 duels covers every possible pair — a complete ranking with no model needed at all. Minis & Docs is 11 shows (55 pairs), Comedy is 15 (105 pairs). Only Drama, at 29, still genuinely needs sampling.

The leagues start from the Type tags already on your database, with one deliberate override:

  • Anime — tagged Anime or Shounen.
  • Minis & Docs — tagged Mini-series, Documentary, or Anthology.
  • Comedy — everything tagged Sitcom, plus the unserious dramas: Sex Education, The OC, Off Campus, Adarsh Baal Vidyalaya, Pritam and Pedro, Dhindhora. These are tagged as drama but you don't watch them the way you watch Rome, and a league only works if everything in it is competing at the same thing.
  • Drama — what's left: prestige, crime, fantasy, superhero.

That override is the whole point of leagues, and it's worth being explicit about: the tag says what a show is, the league says what you were in the mood for. When those disagree, the mood is the better predictor of how you'll judge a duel.

Each league fits its own model, standardised against its own members, so a 7.0 anime is judged against anime and not against Game of Thrones. And because a within-league duel is still a perfectly valid global duel, every answer you give inside a league also sharpens the Everything ladder. Nothing is wasted.

If you want to go further

  • Rate on two axes (how good vs. how much I loved it) and run two ladders. Most "arbitrary" ratings are really two numbers fighting.
  • Model shows with a rewatch count > 1 separately — rewatchability is a distinct signal your single score is currently blending in.
  • Add a tier cutoff: report S/A/B/C tiers from the log-odds cliffs rather than a strict 1–60, since the model itself says most adjacent pairs are coin flips.
